Chapter overview

Innovation opportunities are abundant, but organizations differ in what they notice, where they search, and how well they turn what they find into action. Chapter 7 treats search as a strategic capability: cast the net widely, look through multiple lenses, and build the learning capacity to use new knowledge.

Big idea: Strong innovation search balances competing needs—known and novel, near and far, short and long term—and connects discovery to the ability to absorb and apply knowledge.

1 · Innovation is interaction

Innovation can begin with a technology or knowledge push, a market or user pull, or forces such as regulation. The notes emphasize that innovation rarely comes from push or pull alone: needs often select and shape which knowledge-based possibilities become important, while regulation can both direct innovation and create demand for it.

Knowledge / technology push

New knowledge or capability opens possibilities. Search asks what new applications a capability could enable.

Need / market pull

Users, customers, or unmet needs point toward problems worth solving and help select among possibilities.

Regulation & users

Rules can steer or stimulate change. Users may also invent new solutions themselves, pushing possibility in new directions.

Remember: Ask how push and pull interact in a particular case—not which one is universally more important.

2 · Exploitation, exploration, and ambidexterity

DimensionExploitationExploration
Core questionHow can we do what we already do better?What different direction or new knowledge could change what we do?
Innovation patternIncremental, frequent, small-scale improvement; leverages established knowledge.Radical, occasional, higher-risk search; reaches beyond the current domain.
OrganizationStructured routines, often embedded in operating units.Looser, cross-functional combinations of resources; room for experimentation.
RiskMore familiar and predictable, but can reinforce path dependency.Uncertain and costly, but may open a new trajectory or competitive position.

Most firms need a portfolio: many lower-risk improvements alongside a smaller number of longer-range, higher-risk opportunities. This capacity to work both sides is called ambidexterity. It is difficult because the routines that efficiently exploit existing knowledge can crowd out signals that call for exploration.

Punctuated equilibrium

Innovation often proceeds through extended periods of elaboration within an established trajectory, interrupted by breakthroughs that establish a new one.

Timing and diffusion

Adoption is gradual, often S-shaped: innovative users and early adopters are followed by the majority and then laggards or persistent non-adopters. Early in a technology life cycle, experimentation and product innovation are prominent.

Incumbent trap: Established firms may focus on their current value network, routines, and rewards. Sunk costs and the “sailing ship effect”—intensifying improvement of an older technology when a new one appears—can delay response to emerging alternatives.

3 · Map the innovation search space

Use several complementary lenses. The 4Ps point to the directions in which innovation may occur; distance and time horizons help decide how broadly and how far ahead to search.

4Ps · direction

Product/service offering · Process of creating or delivering it · Position in the market or context of use · Paradigm (the underlying mental model or business logic).

Search lenses · proximity

Microscope: detailed search in the core. Telescope: adjacent opportunities. Radio telescope: peripheral weak signals that may foreshadow transformation. Keep all three in the portfolio.

Also vary the time horizon: maintain attention to the current business, emerging opportunities, and longer-term possibilities. Search should include alternative frames—looking at the organization or industry through fresh eyes.

Four zones of innovation search

The map combines the degree of change (incremental to radical) with the degree of environmental complexity and whether the existing frame is retained or changed.

ZoneSearch logicTypical emphasis
1Business as usualIncremental improvement within a stable, shared frame.Exploit: refine products/processes and deepen established relationships.
2Bounded explorationSearch into new territory, while retaining the accepted business model and frame.Push known frontiers; strategic R&D bets, IP positioning, and technology trajectories.
3Alternative frameReframe the opportunity by combining elements in a different way; a new frame need not mean wholly new technology or markets.Explore alternative architectures and configurations; often requires new networks and a fresh mindset.
4Edge of chaosNew possibilities emerge through coevolution among many interacting elements; no predefined path.Amplification and feedback turn small shifts into a trajectory; a dominant design may eventually emerge.

Zones 1 and 2 search within a familiar frame. Zones 3 and 4 involve alternative architectures or emergent ones, and are harder for established organizations because routines, artifacts, incentives, and dominant assumptions can bound what they see.

4 · Knowledge moves through people

Knowledge is social: people carry, communicate, translate, and combine it. Broad, “cosmopolitan” connections can expose a firm to more sources than a narrow, parochial search.

Knowledge networks
Relationships and clusters through which information and expertise circulate.
Brokers / connectors
People who bridge distinct knowledge worlds and help useful knowledge cross boundaries.
Structural hole
A gap between groups holding different, nonredundant information; a broker can connect them.
Technological gatekeeper
Recognizes relevant external knowledge and has internal connections to route it to the right people.
Allen curve
Communication frequency tends to fall as physical distance between people increases.
Community of practice
A group whose shared work and interaction concentrate specialist knowledge and support learning.
Knowledge architecture
The arrangement of knowledge and relationships that helps a community handle change within an established configuration, but may make a new configuration harder to see.
Knowledge work
Transform tacit know-how, articulate employees’ practical knowledge, and assimilate external knowledge into active use.
Practical implication: Innovation search should mobilize multiple channels: employees, R&D, sales, purchasing, customers, suppliers, and wider networks. Ordinary employees’ experience can reveal improvement opportunities that formal systems miss.

5 · Absorptive capacity: learn to learn

Absorptive capacity (AC) is a firm’s ability to recognize the value of external knowledge, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends. It depends partly on prior related knowledge: a firm is better at recognizing and using knowledge when it has a foundation that helps it understand the new information.

1. Search & acquireFind and access relevant knowledge (potential AC).
2. AssimilateUnderstand and make sense of what has been acquired.
3. TransformCombine new knowledge with what the organization already knows.
4. ExploitApply it in implementation and value creation (realized AC).

Finding knowledge is not enough: potential AC does not automatically become innovation. The links between acquisition, assimilation, transformation, and exploitation matter. AC is not limited to formal R&D or large firms; learning in SMEs and process improvement also count.

Adaptive learning

Establishes and reinforces routines for handling a given level of environmental complexity.

Generative learning

Builds the capacity to take on new levels of complexity and respond to unfamiliar conditions.

Organizations can support search through varied structures, such as balancing applied R&D with “blue sky” work, and by involving employees through suggestion systems, problem-solving groups, innovation jams, intrapreneurship, and tacitly supported “bootlegging.” Strong customer and supplier ties, including supply-chain learning, also help knowledge move.

Check your understanding

Try answering from memory first. Open each prompt to compare your response with the key idea.

1. Why is innovation better explained by push–pull interaction than by choosing one source?

Knowledge and technology create possibilities, while needs can select and shape which possibilities matter. Regulation and user-led problem solving can influence both sides.

2. How do exploitation and exploration differ, and why does a firm need both?

Exploitation improves and leverages known knowledge; exploration seeks new knowledge and directions at greater risk. A portfolio needs reliable improvement and the possibility of breakthrough or renewal—ambidexterity.

3. What do the microscope, telescope, and radio telescope represent?

Search in the core, in adjacent areas, and at the periphery for weak signals. A robust search portfolio balances all three.

4. Distinguish Zones 2 and 3.

Zone 2 explores new territory but within the established frame. Zone 3 changes the frame, seeking alternative architectures and combinations.

5. Why can incumbent firms miss disruptive opportunities?

Established search routines, rewards, sunk costs, and continued improvement of an older technology can keep attention inside the existing value network and delay response to peripheral change.

6. What is the difference between potential and realized absorptive capacity?

Potential AC covers acquiring and assimilating external knowledge. Realized AC adds transformation and exploitation—the capabilities needed to put knowledge to use.

7. Name three ways an organization can widen its knowledge search.

Examples include connecting sales and purchasing to external signals; building relationships with customers and suppliers; using brokers/gatekeepers; enabling employee suggestion and problem-solving; and exploring broader networks.

8. How do adaptive and generative learning complement each other?

Adaptive learning strengthens routines for current complexity; generative learning helps the organization cope with new levels of complexity.

Short-answer synthesis: An effective innovation search strategy maps opportunities across direction (4Ps), distance (core, adjacent, peripheral), time horizons, and search mode (exploit/explore and existing/new frame). It then builds networks and absorptive capacity so discoveries can be recognized, assimilated, transformed, and applied.
