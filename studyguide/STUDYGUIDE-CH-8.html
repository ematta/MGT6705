Chapter overview

Innovation depends on knowledge, and knowledge moves through relationships. Chapter 8 treats innovation as a multiplayer process: firms combine internal expertise with suppliers, users, universities, investors, governments, and other organizations. Effective networks can deliver more than the sum of separate contributions, but they require active coordination and trust.

Big idea: Build and manage relationships that connect complementary knowledge and assets, while keeping the network focused on shared value and learning.

1 · What makes a network?

A network is a system of nodes (people, firms, teams, universities, or other actors) connected by links (relationships and interactions). Its value comes not only from individual ties, but also from the configuration of the whole network: who is connected, what knowledge flows, and where an actor sits in relation to others.

Why network?

Networks help when actors have complementary resources, shared infrastructure or standards, and potential benefits that outweigh the costs of governance. They can reduce uncertainty and transaction costs when full acquisition or arm’s-length purchasing is unsuitable.

How knowledge flows

Innovation knowledge moves through complex “social spaghetti”: people communicate with different colleagues, at different rates, about different issues. Cross-functional teams, gatekeepers, communities of practice, and external links help connect those flows.

As innovation grows more complex, it usually requires more participants, often beyond the firm. This reflects a shift from linear technology-push or demand-pull models toward interactive innovation, supported by communication technologies and boundary-spanning work.

Network fit: A network is useful when collaboration, shared learning, and access to complementary assets create enough value to justify the effort of maintaining the relationships.

2 · Different forms serve different purposes

Networks may emerge from shared interests and interdependence, or be deliberately engineered by a firm or broker. They also differ in openness: closed networks can protect proprietary standards and lock in participants, while open arrangements invite broader exchange.

Network formPurpose
Entrepreneur networksBring together complementary resources, advice, mentoring, and support to advance an opportunity.
Internal teams and employee networksCross organizational boundaries to combine skills, surface employee ideas, and support internal ventures.
Communities of practiceConnect people who share a concern or area of knowledge, sometimes across organizations.
Clusters and sector networksUse geographic proximity or a shared industry to enable exchange, collaboration, and competitiveness.
Development consortiaShare knowledge to create products, processes, technologies, or emerging standards.
User and open innovation networksBring external users and knowledge sources into innovation; innovation markets and crowdfunding can extend the model to solutions, resources, or judgement.

Networks have their own activity cycles and can be unstable as members, interests, or conditions change. Virtual networks can speed information exchange, including supply-chain coordination and customer processing.

3 · Create value together—and scale it

Networks can provide access to diverse and complementary knowledge, share risks, open markets and technologies, and pool skills and assets. They also support shared learning: organizations can adapt practices developed elsewhere rather than reinventing them alone. Supply-chain learning, regional clusters, and precompetitive research consortia are common examples.

Combine

Bring distinct expertise and assets together to create a solution no participant could readily produce alone.

Learn

Share experience, experiment, and adapt effective practices across organizations and communities.

Scale

Coordinate the wider system needed to move a successful pilot toward sustained market or social impact.

Scaling innovation is a long-term, multi-player challenge. Value-network analysis helps map the roles involved: value creators develop new value; consumers use it; captors receive value; conveyors add value in development or delivery; channels provide infrastructure; coordinators connect participants; cartographers shape markets; competitors vie for consumer attention; and complementors enhance the offering.

Emergent value: A network’s combined effects can exceed the sum of individual contributions, but only when organizing energy helps participants coordinate and sustain the work.

4 · Open innovation connects knowledge across boundaries

Open innovation means deliberately searching beyond organizational boundaries and managing knowledge flows into and out of the firm. It rests on the idea that useful expertise is distributed: not all the smart people work for one organization. Firms may use external ideas, license or share technology, partner in development, or bring user knowledge into their process.

Inbound flows

Look outside for ideas, technologies, user insights, and expertise that can strengthen internal innovation.

Outbound flows

Find routes for internally developed knowledge that the firm cannot or will not exploit alone, including licensing and partnerships.

Intermediaries and brokers can bridge organizations, connect problems with solutions, and help partners navigate unfamiliar knowledge or markets. Established relationships support incremental improvement, but they can also narrow attention; “the ties that bind” may become “the ties that blind.”

5 · Find, form, and develop new partnerships

Building a new network involves two distinct tasks: finding relevant partners and forming relationships with them. Finding concerns the breadth of search and ability to see beyond familiar industry assumptions. Forming concerns whether prospective partners are willing to engage and whether the relationship offers mutual benefit.

BarrierChallenge
GeographicalDistance and cultural differences make opportunities in unfamiliar places harder to identify and assess.
TechnologicalOpportunities can arise at the intersection of technology domains that rarely interact.
InstitutionalDifferent origins and objectives, such as public and private sector priorities, can inhibit connection.
IdeologicalDifferent values and norms can obscure opportunities or create friction between partners.
Demographic and ethnicDifferences in needs, values, and cultural experience can impede trust and collaboration.

A useful diagnostic combines partner visibility (easy or difficult to find) with partner willingness (ready or reluctant to engage). Easy-to-find, willing partners are relatively straightforward; hard-to-find partners require broader search; reluctant partners require work on mutual value and trust; and partners who are both hard to find and reluctant require sustained relationship-building.

Practical sequence: Search beyond familiar circles, assess the fit and barriers, build a relationship around mutual benefit, then develop the link into a high-performing partnership.

6 · Manage the network, not only the firm

Network management calls for skills beyond managing owned resources. A network shapes behavior through information flows and through the power differences created by participants’ positions. The challenge is to see system-level effects, maintain shared purpose, and coordinate actors whose interests do not fully align.

Conditions that help

High-performing innovation networks are diverse, proactively managed, and supported by gatekeepers who broker knowledge. Financial leverage and access to investors can distribute risk and provide market intelligence.

Tensions to handle

Build trust and shared risk-taking without excessive contractual friction; address free riders and knowledge spillovers; and prevent narrow self-interest from undermining collective outcomes.

Different network settings call for different management approaches. Similar partners pursuing tactical improvements may need light coordination; diverse partners working on new concepts need active brokering, relationship-building, and shared expectations.

Key terms

Node and link
An actor in a network and the relationship or interaction connecting actors.
Emergent network
A network that forms through interdependence or shared interests.
Engineered network
A network deliberately assembled and developed by a focal organization or broker.
Open innovation
Managing knowledge flows across organizational boundaries to advance innovation.
Gatekeeper
A person or organization that connects participants to knowledge and expertise across boundaries.
Value network
The connected set of roles and relationships that jointly creates and delivers value.
Free rider
A participant who benefits from collective activity without contributing fairly.
Knowledge spillover
Knowledge that flows beyond the intended boundary or recipient, sometimes unintentionally.

Review questions

Why is innovation increasingly described as a networked process?

Complex innovation combines knowledge and assets held by many actors. Networks connect these sources and support interactive learning across and beyond firms.

When is a network preferable to relying only on markets or hierarchy?

When complementary capabilities, shared infrastructure, learning, and uncertainty make collaboration more valuable than arm’s-length transactions or full ownership, after governance costs are considered.

How do open innovation and user networks change the firm’s role?

The firm becomes a manager of knowledge flows and relationships, actively drawing on external ideas and users rather than treating its boundary as the limit of innovation.

What are the two tasks involved in creating new networks?

Finding relevant partners and forming relationships with them; each faces different barriers and requires different capabilities.

What tensions must network managers balance?

Collective system value and individual interests, openness and knowledge protection, trust and formal controls, plus contribution and free riding.
